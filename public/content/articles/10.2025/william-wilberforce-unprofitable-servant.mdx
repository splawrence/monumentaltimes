# The "Unprofitable Servant": William Wilberforce

Tenacity. Courage. Virtue. Winsomeness. Devotion. Integrity. Humility. The enduring trademarks of a man whose accomplishments and fame were more than matched by his character. His nation had forgotten her Christian heritage and was sliding into deeper spiritual and social decay when he realized that God had called him to take a stand. Using all the influence he could command, he strove to bring reform: not merely in cultural norms and laws, but in the very hearts and minds of his countrymen. Resistance was intense and progress slow, but he was faithful to his principles and purposes. And when success eventually crowned his endeavors, he remained untainted by pride, seeing himself as merely an "unprofitable servant" of the Lord.

## Early Life and Conversion

Born in the port city of Hull on August 4, 1759, William was a frail child with weak eyes. Although he battled physical ailments all his life, his lively mind, personal charm, and kind nature were never hampered. After his aunt and uncle exposed him to true Christianity, William's mother whisked him away to a life of frivolity and pleasure, hoping to protect her son from "enthusiasm."

His years at Cambridge and St. John's College were largely frittered away on card parties and idle amusements. Innately witty, charming, and musical, he became the most popular man on campus. Even after his election to Parliament, his pleasure-seeking ways continued, fed by the estate inherited from his father and uncle. He and his friend William Pitt joined clubs, socialized with the elite, and partied as if they were still in college.

Largely due to the force of his magnetic personality and persuasive speech, he influenced Pitt's election to prime minister and won the prestigious Yorkshire seat for himself. At 24, William had a life anyone in England might covet. Yet God used a "coincidence" to radically change his life.

## The Turning Point

After running into friend Isaac Milner, William invited him on a journey to the Franco-Italian Rivera. During this time, William and Isaac discussed spiritual matters. As they talked, William's flippancy turned to interest, then intellectual assent, and finally true belief. He wrestled intensely with his own sinfulness and marveled in the sufficient, atoning work of Christ.

William decided his conversion required withdrawal from government. He wrote to his friend and prime minister, Pitt. Pitt wasn't a Christian, but he challenged William's view, pointing out that Christian principles should lead to action, not only meditation. But, despite Pitt's best efforts to dissuade him, William staunchly held to his new-found conviction.

## Meeting John Newton

However, before making a decision, he sought godly counsel. On December 7, 1785, he met with a former slave-ship captain, John Newton. After his life was transformed by the gospel, John had eventually become a minister, also penning the now-famous hymn "Amazing Grace." John encouraged William to remain in his elected position, suggesting that God had placed him there for the good of the church and nation.

## A Two-Fold Calling

With new consecration to God and a vision for how his parliamentary position could be used, William's eyes were quickly opened to opportunities. On every level, England had spiraled into moral debauchery: immorality, drunkenness, gambling, dueling, prostitution, and corruption were rampant. William began to fight these issues wherever he could. After a small group of abolitionists begged him to become their parliamentary leader, William hesitated feeling a better-qualified person could be found. Yet after spending time praying and studying in his library on a rainy day, he felt an unmistakable call from God. William knew he had a two-fold purpose: the abolition of the slave trade and the reformation of English culture.

## Fighting for Change

Only those who have struggled for change in a cultural mindset can possibly know the heartbreaking, exhausting work God had placed before William. Through his book, *A Practical View of Christianity*, William outlined what he believed England needed most: not a return to a moral standard, a spirit of love and kindness for other people, or a new kind of government. He preached the gospel boldly in his book, showing what a truly Christian life should look like.

He also endeavored to change the social climate in other tangible ways. Not limiting himself to speeches and laws, he founded an organization and enlisted influential men to partner with him. When the king issued a proclamation calling for social reform, William used the opportunity to personally urge civil and church leaders to join the cause.

Any efforts to curtail the slave trade were constantly stymied and voted down. Political power, monetary gain, fear of revolt, and a wariness of anything smelling of fanaticism motivated opposition. But William had a strength of resolve and patient persistence rivaled by few. He quietly whittled away at public opinion with speeches and pamphlets, always taking small victories where he could win them.

## Family and Personal Life

William's strength of character wasn't limited to his public life. After a whirlwind courtship, William married Barbara Spooner on May 30, 1797 and they had two girls and three boys. Extremely devoted, he delighted in his family and encouraged them to pursue the Lord. When he felt his position kept him away from them too much, he resigned his seat in Yorkshire for one with less importance and responsibility.

His relationship with God remained the mainspring of his activity and he spent hours in study and prayer, creating lists of friends he was praying for. He also jotted notes about ways to have spiritual conversations with them. He cut out amusements that distracted him or wasted time and journaled about his spiritual life. Gregarious and charming to the end, his fun-loving, agreeable nature was tempered with a consecration that left an impression on all who knew him.

## Victory and Legacy

Through long years of poor health, disappointments, uncertainty, and setbacks, he continued to faithfully pursue what God had set before him. His influence brought great change in England as he championed for reform in prisons, legal punishment, factories, elections, and more. And after a decades-long fight, he saw the slave trade abolished by law on March 25, 1807. Tremendously thankful, he then turned his attention to the institution of slavery.

William retired from Parliament in 1825 due to failing health. He spent his last years living with one of his children after paying his son's debt incurred from a failed business venture. But leaving public life never removed him from the fight. He kept up correspondence and gave occasional speeches, giving his last address a mere three months before his death.

As he lay on his deathbed in 1833, word was brought to him of the abolition of slavery and the emancipation of the slaves, a fitting sendoff to the champion of the slaves.

As news of the bill spread through the English empire, William Wilberforce left this world on July 29th. Generations later, he is still heralded as a great social reformer and man of faith, undoubtedly hearing his Creator declare "Well done, good and faithful servant."
